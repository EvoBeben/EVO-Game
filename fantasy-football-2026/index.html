2026 Season · Draft Guide · Verified 25 July 2026

Everything you need before you're on the clock

The Seahawks are champions, Mahomes is coming off a torn ACL, A.J. Brown is a Patriot, and the top of the draft board is a two-man argument between Detroit and Atlanta. Here is the whole 2026 landscape — strategy, tiers, names, rookies, and the late-round guys who actually win leagues.

01 — Where we're standing

The state of play

Every draft is really an argument about last season. So start with what actually happened, because most of the 2026 board is priced off it.

Seattle won Super Bowl LX on 8 February, beating New England 29–13 behind a defense that sacked Drake Maye six times and forced three turnovers. Running back Kenneth Walker III took MVP — the first back to do it since Terrell Davis — and then promptly left in free agency for Kansas City. Jaxon Smith-Njigba came out of that season as Offensive Player of the Year and enters 2026 as a consensus top-five fantasy receiver.

On the fantasy ledger, Christian McCaffrey was the highest-scoring player in the game on a genuinely absurd workload: 403 touches, including his first 300-carry season (311). Josh Allen led quarterbacks at 374.6 points. And the season's single biggest league-winner relative to cost was Trevor Lawrence, which is a useful reminder that the guy who decides your title is usually someone you got in the last five rounds.

Three structural facts shape this year's board:

  • Running back is expensive again. There are roughly a dozen legitimate workhorses, and ADP has pushed them up. That squeezes the classic Zero-RB build harder than it's been squeezed in years.
  • The rookie class is skill-position thin outside a small handful of names — which is exactly why the veteran backs got repriced upward.
  • Two of the sport's biggest names are injury questions. Patrick Mahomes tore his ACL and LCL in Week 15; Malik Nabers is on his second procedure since a complicated ACL rehab. Both are draftable. Neither is safe.

How to read this page

Rankings are consensus-leaning, not a single site's board — where credible outlets disagree (and at tight end this year, they really disagree), the disagreement is shown rather than smoothed over. Round ranges reflect mid-to-late July ADP and will move through August. Treat this as the map, not the territory: re-check any injury designation the morning of your draft.

02 — The offseason

Eleven moves that rewrote the board

Target share is a zero-sum game. When a 120-target receiver walks out of a building, that volume doesn't evaporate — it gets redistributed, and the people who figure out where first are the ones drafting value.

A.J. Brown WR PHI → NE · for a 2028 1st + 2027 5th

The headline trade of the offseason, reuniting Brown with Mike Vrabel. Brown gives Drake Maye a genuine alpha; the bigger fantasy consequence is in Philadelphia, where DeVonta Smith inherits a receiver room with almost no competition and is the most obvious breakout bet on the board.

D.J. Moore WR CHI → BUF · Moore + a 5th for a 2nd

Buffalo finally bought Josh Allen a proven perimeter receiver. Chicago's offense condenses to three mouths — Rome Odunze, Luther Burden III, Colston Loveland — which is the cleanest projection change of the entire offseason.

Jaylen Waddle WR MIA → DEN · for a 1st + 3rd, swap of 4ths

Waddle moves from an offense that ranked 29th in neutral passing rate to one that ranked 4th. He becomes a real WR2 rather than a boom-bust flex, Bo Nix's ceiling goes up, and Miami becomes a one-man show for De'Von Achane.

Mike Evans WR TB → SF · 3 yrs, up to $60.4M

Twelve years in Tampa, over. Evans joins a 49ers room that also added Christian Kirk, alongside Ricky Pearsall. Downstream: Emeka Egbuka becomes the unambiguous No. 1 in Tampa Bay.

Michael Pittman Jr. WR IND → PIT · 3 yrs, $59M

Pittman gets paid in Pittsburgh. In Indianapolis, Josh Downs is the direct beneficiary and belongs in the WR2 conversation with a healthy Daniel Jones throwing.

Kenneth Walker III RB SEA → KC · free agency

The Super Bowl MVP left for Kansas City after Seattle declined the franchise tag. His fantasy value now rides on Mahomes' knee and a Chiefs offense that has to prove it can run.

Travis Etienne Jr. RB JAX → NO · 4 yrs, $52M

Jacksonville's backfield is now Bhayshul Tuten's, which is the single most exploitable price on the board (more below). New Orleans also drafted a receiver 8th overall, so this is a real offense now.

Kenneth Gainwell RB PIT → TB · free agency

Coming off a career-high 1,023 scrimmage yards, Gainwell lands as the 1B to Bucky Irving's 1A on a team that has always fed its backs in the passing game. He is going around pick 103.

Chig Okonkwo TE TEN → WAS · free agency

Vacates the Titans' tight end job entirely, handing it to second-year Gunnar Helm at a double-digit-round price.

Fernando Mendoza QB Indiana → LV · No. 1 overall

The Heisman winner goes first to the Raiders. Rookie QBs rarely win you a redraft league, but Mendoza has streaming-QB2 value in the right matchups and matters enormously for Brock Bowers' target floor.

Jeremiyah Love RB Notre Dame → ARI · Rd 1

The highest-drafted running back since Saquon Barkley. Arizona still rosters James Conner and Trey Benson, but a first-round back is a first-round back — Love is the rookie most likely to finish top-15 at the position.

03 — How to draft

Strategy, honestly

Most draft advice is a strategy looking for a season. The better habit is a set of principles that survive whatever falls to you at pick 4.7.

Draft tiers, not ranks

The difference between your No. 14 receiver and your No. 15 receiver is noise. The difference between the last man in a tier and the first man in the next one is real. Build your board as blocks, and let the board tell you when to move: if a tier has one name left and the next tier is five deep, take the last man standing. If a tier still has six names, take the scarce position instead and come back.

This single habit fixes most of the errors that cost people leagues — reaching a round early on a guy you'd have gotten anyway, and panic-drafting a position because two went off the board in front of you.

The RB question in 2026, specifically

Zero RB has been the smart-money build for several seasons, and it is genuinely less appealing this year. Two things changed: there are about a dozen true workhorse backs (a healthier count than recent years), and the incoming rookie class is thin at the position, so there's no cheap injury-replacement pipeline waiting in August. Fading the position entirely means betting on a waiver market that may not exist.

That doesn't make Zero RB dead — it makes it situational. Use it when the room drafts backs irrationally early and elite receivers and tight ends fall to you. Don't use it as a pre-committed plan.

The practical version

Hero RB is the default this year. Take one workhorse in the first two rounds, then hammer receiver through the middle, then attack the backfield again from Round 6 onward where the standalone-value backs live. It gives you the RB1 anchor Zero RB lacks and the receiver depth Robust RB gives up.

Robust RB, if you insist

Three-plus backs inside five rounds. It is harder to run in 2026 than it was in 2024 precisely because the position is priced up — you're paying full retail on all three. It works best from the turn (picks 1–3 or 10–12), where you can double-tap, and it obligates you to be aggressive on receivers in Rounds 6–10 rather than drifting into "best available."

Positional timing

  • Quarterback: the late-round approach still wins on points-per-dollar. There are four or five arms outside the top eight who have already produced top-10 seasons. Take two of them in the last five rounds and stream the better matchup.
  • Tight end: genuinely bifurcated. Either pay the Round 2 price for one of the elite two, or wait until Round 10+ and take two shots. The Round 5–8 tight ends are the worst value on the board.
  • Defense and kicker: last two picks. Always. Every year. Stream defenses on matchup; never roster two.

Best ball differences

If you're drafting best ball rather than managed redraft, three things change. Finish with 6–7 running backs, because you have no waiver wire and injuries are absolute. Correlate — pair a quarterback with one or two of his own pass-catchers so a big game scores twice for you. And if you go Zero RB in best ball, an elite tight end is close to non-negotiable; it frees a roster spot you'd otherwise burn on tight end lottery tickets.

What to actually do in an auction

Nominate players you don't want early, while everyone still has money. Budget roughly 60–65% of your cap on your top four roster spots and refuse to spend more than $2 on quarterback, defense and kicker combined. The winning auction habit is boring: be the second-highest bidder on stars and the highest bidder on the tier breaks nobody's paying attention to.

05 — Running back

The backfield board

The 1.01 is a two-man argument, and reasonable people are on both sides of it.

Jahmyr Gibbs has averaged 364.5 fantasy points (21.4 per game) over the past two seasons, finishing RB1 and RB3 — and David Montgomery is no longer in Detroit, which points at the largest workload of his career. Detroit also draws the easiest fantasy schedule for running backs this year. Bijan Robinson has finished RB3 and RB2 in the same span and, critically, has never missed a game. Gibbs is the ceiling pick; Bijan is the floor pick. Gibbs and Robinson are the only two backs to finish top-four in each of the last two seasons.

Christian McCaffrey is the third name and the hardest call in the first round. He was the highest-scoring fantasy player alive last season — on 403 touches. Every projection system loves him and every risk model flinches. He is a defensible pick and a defensible fade, which is exactly what a top-five ADP with 300+ carries behind it should look like.

06 — Wide receiver

The receiver board

The top of this position is unusually stable, which means the money is made in Rounds 4 through 9, where four separate teams just handed a vacated target tree to somebody.

Ja'Marr Chase remains the gold standard — top-five in receiver points per game in four of his five seasons, and a healthy Joe Burrow gives him as good a claim to the overall WR1 finish as anyone. Puka Nacua and Jaxon Smith-Njigba are the unquestioned focal points of their offenses, with Smith-Njigba arriving as reigning Offensive Player of the Year off a championship. Amon-Ra St. Brown centers one of the league's most productive offenses. All four belong in the first five receivers off the board.

The second tier is about volume that survives circumstance. CeeDee Lamb and Justin Jefferson are the safest bets at the position for the same boring reason: elite target volume travels regardless of who's under center. Jefferson was the WR2 last season at 18.7 PPR points per game with Sam Darnold throwing — quarterback anxiety has been a bad reason to fade him for five straight years.

07 — Tight end

Tight end: pay up or wait it out

This is the position where the industry genuinely can't agree, and that disagreement is worth more to you than a fake consensus.

PFF has Trey McBride and Brock Bowers as the top two. CBS puts Bowers first, expecting him to lead the position in targets. Sports Illustrated ranks McBride first with Tyler Warren second and Bowers third. NFL.com's Dan Parr goes Bowers, then Colston Loveland, then McBride. When four credible shops produce four different orders, the honest read is: these are all the same pick, take whichever falls.

The case for Bowers is situational — Klint Kubiak arriving from the Super Bowl-winning Seattle staff, a veteran quarterback to open the year, and a thin receiver room around him. He saw 150-plus targets last year and has a real shot at McBride's single-season tight end reception record (126). The case for McBride is that he already was the No. 1 fantasy tight end last season.

Both cost a second-round pick. That is a lot. The alternative — and it is a real one — is to skip the position entirely until Round 10 and take two of the double-digit-round names below.

08 — Quarterback

Quarterback: the position you should not pay for

Twelve quarterbacks will finish as usable weekly starters. There are twelve teams in your league. Do the arithmetic and then draft accordingly.

The consensus top of the position runs Josh Allen, Joe Burrow, Drake Maye, Lamar Jackson and Jayden Daniels, with Jaxson Dart, Brock Purdy, Jalen Hurts, Matthew Stafford, Dak Prescott, Caleb Williams and Trevor Lawrence filling out the top twelve. Allen adding D.J. Moore is the single most bullish arrow in the group.

The two bounce-back cases are the interesting ones. Lamar Jackson managed only 17.1 points per game last season and finished QB16 — but that was an injury-wrecked year, he was still QB5 in passing points per attempt, and he is one season removed from being the overall QB1. Jayden Daniels was QB5 in points per game as a rookie (20.9), lost 2025 to injury, and now enters a new system with more under-center work, play-action and pre-snap motion. Both are being drafted below what a healthy version is worth.

The rule that makes this section work

You cannot roster all of these. Pick four or five, weighted toward the ones whose mechanism is already true rather than the ones that need an injury. Then be ruthless — a deep sleeper who isn't playing by Week 3 is a roster spot, not a strategy.

12 — Fades

Priced wrong the other way

A bust isn't a bad player — it's a good player at a bad price. Avoiding them is arguably the single biggest factor in winning a redraft league, and it's less fun than finding sleepers, which is exactly why fewer people do it.

The cautionary tale to keep in mind all August is Brian Thomas Jr. Twelve months ago he was one of the fastest-rising names on draft boards, projected as the WR1 of a pass-heavy Jacksonville offense and widely called one of the safest high-upside picks available. The Jaguars traded for Jakobi Meyers midseason, and Thomas finished as the WR45. Consensus enthusiasm is not the same thing as a secured role.

13 — Between now and Week 1

The August watchlist

Camps opened in waves — Seattle brought rookies in on 17 July, Arizona and Carolina followed on 22 July, veterans across the league reported 24 July, and every roster was full by 28 July. These are the situations that will move ADP most between now and kickoff.

  • 01Patrick Mahomes' knee. Cleared to practice and a full participant, with Week 1 against Denver on Monday night as the stated goal. Every Chiefs pass-catcher and Kenneth Walker III are priced off this.
  • 02Malik Nabers' ramp-up. Avoided the PUP list, which is the good version of this news — but a four-to-five-game absence is still live, and Jaxson Dart's QB value moves with it.
  • 03Zach Charbonnet's return timeline. On PUP with a playoff ACL tear and no set date. Every week of ambiguity raises Jadarian Price's floor.
  • 04The Jacksonville backfield split. Tuten is the lead back on paper. Chris Rodriguez signed there and out-earned Rachaad White on the open market, which is not nothing.
  • 05Rashee Rice's availability. Post-surgery knee plus off-field resolution. His Round 2–3 ADP assumes a full season.
  • 06Chicago's route distribution. Odunze, Burden and Loveland now split a full target tree three ways. Whoever wins the summer wins a lot of fantasy points.
  • 07Brian Thomas Jr.'s role. Trade rumors circled all spring and the GM publicly called them "fraudulent." His WR31 price is a bet on a role that is not yet visible.
  • 08New England's passing game. A.J. Brown, Drake Maye and a Super Bowl loss to build on. If this clicks, Brown is a Round 1 value at a Round 2 price.
  • 09Arizona's backfield. A first-round rookie in a room that still holds James Conner and Trey Benson. Preseason snap distribution matters here more than almost anywhere.
  • 10Any tight end who wins a job outright. The Round 11+ tier is deep this year; one of those names becomes a weekly starter by September, and it's usually decided in August.

Draft-morning checklist

  1. Re-pull ADP the day of your draft. The numbers on this page are from mid-to-late July and August moves them.
  2. Check the injury report for every player in your top 40 — not the ranking, the designation.
  3. Print your tiers, not your ranks. Cross names off as they go.
  4. Know your league's scoring cold. Half-PPR versus full PPR moves pass-catching backs a full round.
  5. Decide in advance which two of the injury-discount names you're willing to own. Then own no more than one.
  6. Take your last two picks on defense and kicker. Not one pick earlier.
